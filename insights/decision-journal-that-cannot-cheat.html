A decision journal that cannot quietly rewrite the past

A journal teaches only when the prediction existed before the answer and the failure rule was stated before the result.

Ordinary decision journals have a hidden weakness: the same person who made the forecast can later edit its wording, change the interpretation or remember a different level of confidence. A useful journal must protect the past from the present.

Lock four things before new evidence arrives

  1. The claim. Use a precise outcome, population and time window.
  2. The falsifier. State what result counts as failure.
  3. The scope. Record where the prediction does and does not apply.
  4. The evidence cutoff. List what was available when the prediction was made.

A confidence number without a falsifier is decoration. “I am 80% confident this product will work” allows too many escapes. “Within 30 days, at least 20% of invited users will complete the defined task; below 10% falsifies the adoption claim” is auditable.

Use an external timestamp

A local file date can be edited. A hash of the prediction artifact registered with an independent timestamping service is stronger. The service does not need the private text; it can timestamp a cryptographic digest. Later, the original artifact can be hashed again and compared with the registered digest.

The registration time—not a self-reported “created at” field—should be the decisive lock. If evidence was already public before that time, the observation is retrospective and must not be counted as a prospective calibration result.

Count claims, not correction events

If one claim is corrected three times, that is still one claim with a history. A failure rate should divide the number of resolved claims containing a substantive reversal by the number of eligible resolved claims. Counting correction events in both numerator and denominator can manufacture a meaningless rate of one.

Refuse to calculate when the sample is too small

A system should be allowed to say “insufficient history.” Five resolved claims are still a fragile basis for a frequency estimate, but they are more honest than one. Report a wide uncertainty interval and do not let a global average bypass a minimum sample requirement applied to individual categories.

The most important feature may be silence: if predictions were reconstructed after outcomes were known, or decisive evidence was undated, return no calibration rate.

Preserve every correction

Never delete a failed claim. Append an event explaining what changed, which evidence caused the change and what type of error occurred. A fixed vocabulary—scope mismatch, unsupported assumption, sign error, new primary source—makes patterns visible over time.

Prospective record
  • locked claim and falsifier;
  • artifact hash and independent registration time;
  • dated evidence references;
  • append-only corrections;
  • eligibility gate before any rate is shown.

This discipline is slower than storytelling and much faster than repeatedly trusting a system that has never measured its own failures.
